## 1. Executive summary

Digital Fortuna Computing (DFC), founded by Hanson Peprah, is a Ghanaian technology company, not yet registered, that runs a marketplace where engineers and entrepreneurs buy and sell complete startups, SaaS products and AI platforms (in the way Acquire.com and Flippa do), and that builds AI tools of its own. Its founder also builds Lidapay, a live app for airtime, data and local shopping, under Advansis Technologies. AdGenie, its AI ad platform, generates ad images, video and copy for TikTok, Meta and other platforms. The company therefore has four revenue lines: the marketplace (starting with its own listings), AdGenie, a done-for-you marketing service, and from 2027 a managed AI back-office offering built on the JARVIS agent system, with industrial agents for mining, oil and gas and cement to follow.

The strategy is to win a narrow, underserved market first: African small and medium businesses that sell through social media and WhatsApp. These customers need fast, affordable, locally relevant ads, and the global AI ad tools were not built for them. Revenue comes from three connected lines: AdGenie subscriptions and credits, retainer-based marketing services, and managed JARVIS deployments.

The near-term goal is a focused, honest public launch before the end of 2026, real paying customers in Q1 2027, and a fundable or sellable business by mid-2027.

**Why now.** Ghana had about 24.3 million internet users in January 2025, 1.87 million business establishments in 2024, and roughly GH¢4.5 trillion of mobile money transactions in 2025 (provisional). Most of those businesses are small and already sell through social media and mobile money, yet the AI ad tools available to them are priced and designed for Western buyers. Existing AI ad tools are priced in USD, built for Western brands and languages, and do not connect to local payment and messaging habits.

Opportunity:

- Large and growing base of social-first SMEs in fashion, beauty, food, events, real estate and fintech.
- Low-cost content creation is the pain point, with ad spend following once content works.
- Few local competitors combine AI creative, local languages and WhatsApp-led sales flows.

## 5. Positioning and moat

Do not compete head-on with well-funded global tools. Differentiate on:

- **Local relevance.** Templates, offers and styles built for African SMEs.
- **Language.** Copy and voiceover in Twi, Pidgin, Hausa, Yoruba, Swahili and French.
- **WhatsApp-first flows.** Click-to-chat ads and conversation templates that close sales.
- **Local payments.** Mobile money and cedi billing alongside USD cards.
- **Trust and security.** Bank-grade engineering practices, valuable when selling to fintechs and regulated firms.
- **Service plus software.** Customers who want results, not tools, can buy the managed option.

## 6. Target customers and markets

## 7. Business model and pricing

Move from "unlimited" plans to credit-based pricing.

- **Free:** small number of generations, email verification required, watermarked outputs.
- **Starter / Pro:** monthly credit allowance with top-up packs.
- **Business:** team seats, brand kits, priority processing, API access.
- **Managed services:** monthly retainer by scope.
- **JARVIS setups:** one-time setup fee plus monthly maintenance.

**Provisional price points (ex-VAT).** Starter $12 (GH¢141), Growth $29 (GH¢340) and Business $79 (GH¢926) a month, against competitor entry prices of about $20 to $39. They rest on published competitor prices and an assumed credit cost, and both will move once you have provider invoices and pilot data. Gross margin follows from the worksheet:

```latex
\text{gross margin} = \frac{\text{price} - \text{AI cost} - \text{payment fees}}{\text{price}}
```

The sensitivity is steep. If AI cost is the only cost, the margin you keep is simple to read off.

| AI cost as a share of price | Margin before payment fees |
| --- | --- |
| 20% | 80% |
| 40% | 60% |
| 60% | 40% |
| 80% | 20% |

Decide the lowest margin you will accept before you set any price, then set credits so the heaviest plausible user stays above it.

**Abuse and cost controls.** Verify email, require a payment method for video, cap daily generations per account, queue video jobs, and alert when spend per user passes a limit.

```latex
\text{runway (months)} = \frac{\text{cash in the bank}}{\text{monthly net burn}}
```

## 13. Funding strategy: how to get investors

I am not a lawyer or financial advisor, so treat this as a framework and check specifics with qualified advisers.

**What investors fund.** At your stage, investors back evidence: paying customers, growth, retention and a founder who can execute. A live app and a good deck are not enough on their own. The strongest move is to arrive with revenue, even small, and a clear story of what it will grow into.

**Sequence.**

1. **Customers first.** Reach your first paying customers and pilot results. This also funds you and gives you leverage.
2. **Non-dilutive and low-dilution options.** Accelerators and programmes such as Y Combinator, Techstars, MEST, and Google for Startups Africa, plus grants and competitions. Check each programme's current eligibility and deadlines.
3. **Angels.** Founders and operators in your fintech and capital-markets network, diaspora investors, and pan-African angel networks. Warm introductions convert far better than cold messages.
4. **Pre-seed funds.** Only after you can show traction. Early funds typically want a clear niche, repeatable acquisition and a path to scale beyond one country.

**Ask size.** The model supports a raise of US$100,000 (about GH¢1.17 million) for the funded growth plan. The bootstrapped base case needs only about $5,000 (GH¢58,600) as a buffer, so the raise buys speed, not survival: hiring, a bigger marketing budget and entry into Nigeria and French-speaking markets. Do not state a valuation until advisers and comparable deals support one.

## 14. Exit options

Acquire.com, Flippa, Empire Flippers and FE International list online businesses for sale, and they are also the marketplace line's direct competitors (chapter 17); selling a product on a competitor is an option, but listing it on your own marketplace first is the plan. Buyers generally want proven recurring revenue, documented operations, clean code and a business that can run without the founder. A product with no revenue sells for little, so build revenue first. Strategic acquisition by a marketing platform or agency network is another route once you have a distinct niche and customer base.

**What the marketplaces actually serve.** One 2026 review of live Acquire.com listings and closed deals describes a market of operator buyers and small search funds shopping businesses under $5 million, with most deals closing under $2 million ([Beancount.io guide](https://beancount.io/uk/blog/2026/07/11/bootstrapped-saas-valuation-multiples-2026-acquire-com-indie-founders-guide)). That is a secondary source, and I could not confirm specific valuation multiples for very small software businesses from a primary dataset, so this plan quotes none. Get live comparables from the platforms themselves before setting any price expectation.

**Exit routes compared.**

| Route | Likely buyer | What they pay for | What you need ready |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Online marketplace sale (Acquire.com, Flippa, Empire Flippers, FE International) | Operators, small funds, individual buyers | Proven recurring revenue, profit, clean handover | Several months of steady revenue, clean accounts, documented operations, transferable code |
| Strategic sale | Marketing platforms, agencies, payment or telecom firms in Africa | Customers, local-language capability, distribution | A distinct niche, retained customers, a team or documentation that survives your exit |
| Acqui-hire | Larger tech firms | Founder and team | A track record and a product that works |
| Keep and grow | None | Cash flow and control | Margin and retention strong enough to fund growth |

**Sale-readiness checklist.** Monthly revenue and churn for at least several months; AI and hosting costs tracked per customer; code in a repository the company owns, with licence confirmations; security summary; documented procedures for support, billing and deployment; customer contracts and consents; a clear story of why the business can run without you.

A sale is a realistic outcome only after revenue is proven. Until then the work is the same as for investors: real customers, measured results and clean paperwork.

**How to win the first clients.**

1. Start with service companies, contractors, engineering firms and cement distributors, not the largest operators. They decide faster and still feel the pain.
2. Pitch the agent layer as a complement to systems they already own, not a replacement for them.
3. Lead with the local-content angle where the policy applies, and with a clean security and data-residency story: the platform runs in their network, and your fintech security record is the proof point.
4. Win two or three reference sites, then publish measured results (hours saved, downtime days avoided, filings made on time).
5. Build ISO 27001-style certification early; industrial buyers ask for it.
6. Work through industry bodies such as the Ghana Chamber of Mines and the Ghana Oil and Gas Service Providers Association for introductions.

**Risks specific to this vertical.**

- Long sales cycles and slow payment, modelled as 75 receivable days.
- Safety liability: stay read-only and keep humans in every decision.
- Sensitive data: production and geological data need private deployment, access controls and clear contracts.
- Licence: JARVIS permits commercial use for clients but not reselling its source code. Get written confirmation for industrial deployments, and plan to own the agent layer you build on top.
- Regulatory change: the Minerals and Mining Act is under review, which adds work but can also create demand.
- Competition from global industrial software suites and equipment makers' own monitoring services. Your edge is price, speed and local presence, not feature breadth.

## 23. Language: making JARVIS and AdGenie speak Twi

**Why it matters.** Akan is the largest ethnic grouping in Ghana, about 45.6% of the population in the 2021 census ([Modern Ghana](https://www.modernghana.com/news/1530553/many-peoples-one-nation-ghanas-rich-ethnic.amp)), and around 80% of Ghanaians are reported to speak it as a first or second language ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_language)). Its literary varieties are Asante Twi, Akuapem Twi and Fante. Akan is the native language of much of the southern half of the country and a widely understood trading language beyond it, so it is understood across many of the 16 regions even where it is not the mother tongue. A business assistant that works in Twi reaches customers that English-only tools miss.

**What already exists, so we build on it.**

| Resource | What it offers | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GhanaNLP corpora | 41,513 sentence pairs across Twi, Fante, Ewe, Ga and Kusaal | [arXiv, March 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13793) |
| Khaya AI | Translation, speech recognition and text-to-speech for 30+ African languages through an API and Python SDK | [Khaya SDK](https://khaya-sdk.readthedocs.io/) |
| Akan language models | ABENA and BAKO for Akuapem and Asante Twi; an English to Akuapem Twi corpus of 25,421 pairs | [CatalyzeX listing](https://www.catalyzex.com/author/Anokye%20Acheampong%20Amponsah) |
| Open Twi speech models | One fine-tune reports a 27.4% word error rate in a health-conversation trial, trained on Google WaxalNLP and Common Voice data | [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/teckedd/gha-dondo-w2v-bert-twi-v2) |
| Google Translate | Supports Twi; a Ghanaian team is comparing its own Twi speech model with Google's | [LinkedIn post](https://gh.linkedin.com/in/alhassan-prince) |

Quality in Twi varies a lot by task, so nothing here is assumed to work until we test it.

**What we would build.**

| Product | Twi feature | Where it earns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AdGenie | Twi ad copy and voiceover for local sellers | Subscription credits |
| JARVIS WhatsApp agent | Replies to customers in Twi, including voice notes | Setups and care plans |
| Marketplace | Twi guidance for sellers and buyers | Listings and deals |
| Industrial agents | Twi briefings for crews and community messages, reviewed by a human | Industrial subscriptions |
| Onboarding | Twi tutorials for first-time users | Lower churn |

**How the agent does it.** JARVIS calls a translation and speech service (for example Khaya) as a tool, keeps a glossary for each industry (marketing, finance, mining, cement), and passes every important output to a human reviewer. Over time, consented recordings and corrected translations become our own test set and training data.

**Plan.**

| Phase | When | What | Done when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0: benchmark | Oct to Dec 2026 | Test three or four systems on 100 sentences in each of four domains with 5 native-speaker raters | A scorecard by task and dialect |
| 1: pilot | Q1 2027 | Twi ad copy and WhatsApp replies for pilot customers, human-reviewed | Pilot users choose Twi and rate it at least 4 out of 5 |
| 2: depth | 2027 | Domain glossaries, voice, Fante, and partners such as GhanaNLP and university linguists | A published evaluation set |
| 3: scale | 2028 onward | Ga and Ewe, grants, and fine-tuning on consented data | Twi as a paid feature on every plan |

**Phase 0 budget (assumptions).** Native-speaker raters, 5 people for 40 hours at $5 an hour, $1,000 (GH¢11,720); recordings from 20 speakers for 2 hours each at $10 an hour, $400 (GH¢4,688); API and test credits $200 (GH¢2,344). Total about $1,600 (GH¢18,752). This is not yet in the financial model, so it adds about $1,600 to the Q4 2026 launch costs; later phases fit inside the expansion and R&D budget.

**Measures.** Native-speaker quality score (target at least 4 out of 5 on 80% of items), speech word error rate (target 25% or less on our own test set), share of pilot users who choose Twi, and cost per Twi interaction.

**Risks.** Dialects differ in spelling and tone, data is thin, and an AI can state something false or insulting with confidence. Never claim fluency before testing; use human review for anything public or sensitive; get consent for voice data under the Data Protection Act; and partner with GhanaNLP and universities rather than compete with them.
